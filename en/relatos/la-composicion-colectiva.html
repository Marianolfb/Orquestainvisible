Collective composition and co-authorship

Buenos Aires, 2026 — By Rodrigo Almonacid
© Cari Aimé

Included in Julio's proposal for the Orquesta Invisible was the idea of creating new compositions collaboratively. So, during one of the rehearsals, he suggested putting together a tango, where he provided guidelines and anyone who could contributed a melody or rhythm from their instrument. With what was gathered, he returned to the next rehearsal with a more shaped idea, and "Centenera" began to take form—the orchestra's first collectively composed tango. Ideas continued to be contributed, and Coviello returned to subsequent rehearsals with scores for each instrument, until one day we premiered it at La Tierra Invisible, and it was pure joy.

Included in Julio's proposal for the Orquesta Invisible was the idea of creating new compositions collaboratively.

The week after "Centenera" premiered, Julio sent a message to the group saying we needed to start a new composition and asked for ideas. Mariano (bandoneonist) suggested something more rhythmic, with more notes, like a milonga. That's when I dared to show something I had written years ago—an attempt at a candombe that had several parts but lacked a solid structure. Fernando (viola) mentioned he had a milonga too, so we sent them to the director. He arrived at the rehearsal with a general draft where he had arranged the structure between my candombe and Fer's milonga. We began playing it, recording it with a phone while Julio asked for ideas, and thus the second collectively composed piece began to take shape. Others contributed counterpoints, Mariano wrote a variation, Patricia (pianist) proposed a modulation for a specific moment, and while we rehearsed, the strings began improvising voices and effects unique to their instruments that eventually became part of the arrangement.

At our next performance at La Tierra Invisible, we premiered the candombe under the name "Subterráneo" (the name I had given it when I wrote it 10 years earlier). It was well-received by the audience, but when we announced the title and referenced it as a genre from our Uruguayan brothers, someone in the crowd laughed and said, "Subway (subterráneo) in Uruguay, of all places." I felt quite guilty and spent several days thinking the title had to change. I suggested finding another name to the orchestra, gave my reasons, and everyone agreed. While cycling, I thought of names that showed we were approaching the genre with respect—clichés like "Crossing the River," another like "From Here but From There." Then I remembered a lovely story our director Coviello told us while we were struggling to play the candombe: "This candombe key is called the white key (clave blanca). Do you know why? Because it's a purely Afro-descendant genre, and when it became popular in Uruguay, people wanted to follow the rhythm but couldn't manage the one the Black community did. So they were taught a much simpler one, the one we all know, and the Afro-descendants called it the white key, because it's the rhythm white people can do." With this story in mind, I thought of naming it "Clave Blanca"—a way of saying we perform this genre to the best of our understanding.

This candombe key is called the white key; do you know why they call it that?

In following rehearsals, the repertoire grew with Coviello's themes until, past mid-year, the director proposed recording an album with everything we had by the end of the year. We all agreed and began looking for a studio that could fit 10 people playing simultaneously.

During a conversation, I mentioned to Julio that I had some pieces I'd written long ago but couldn't figure out how to finish. Seeing how the candombe turned out after he worked on it, I liked the idea of handing him the tangos to see what could be done. After one of the concerts at La Tierra Invisible, I told him I'd like to include my track on the album. The next day he asked for the files; I sent two pieces, one from 2015 and another from 2020. We met at his house, and he showed me that he had merged the two into one—they were in the same key with very similar harmony. We went to the piano, and he mentioned they had rock-like harmonies, which wasn't a bad thing since that's the music we grew up listening to. Then he began searching for a harmonic sequence closer to tango, humming a melody as it occurred to him.

...we went to the piano and he spoke to me about harmonies being rather rock-oriented, which wasn't bad because it's the music we grew up with...

That's how "Ceniza" began to emerge; within two weeks, the new tango was almost entirely finished. This all happened at the end of October. By November, the recording studio was chosen and the date confirmed. In less than a month, we premiered it at La Tierra Invisible, and a few weeks later, we were recording it.
